When we only have a sample, we do not know the true population mean, so we calculate the sample mean from the observations themselves.

That mean is positioned to fit that particular sample. As a result, the observations tend to be closer to their own sample mean than they are to the true population mean.

So the squared deviations — and therefore the measured spread — tend to be too small.

Dividing by n − 1 instead of n makes the estimate larger to compensate.

Once the sample mean is known, the deviations from that mean must add to 0.

Notice that the fifth deviation is not free to vary. Once the first four are chosen, the fifth value is forced to be whatever makes the deviations add to zero.

With 5 observations, only 4 deviations are free to vary.

In general, n observations give n − 1 independent deviations. This is why the denominator becomes n − 1.

Bessel's correction makes the estimate of variance unbiased. After taking the square root to obtain standard deviation, a small bias remains, but using n − 1 greatly reduces the underestimation.
